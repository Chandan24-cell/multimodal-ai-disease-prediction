Pulmonary edema is characterized by excessive extravascular fluid in the lungs, often producing bilateral perihilar opacities, vascular redistribution, and diffuse interstitial or alveolar infiltrates. On radiographs, cardiogenic edema typically shows cephalization of pulmonary vessels, Kerley B lines, and symmetric lower lobe or diffuse alveolar shadowing. Noncardiogenic edema can appear similar but is often associated with acute lung injury, sepsis, or drug toxicity without marked cardiomegaly.

The differential diagnosis includes diffuse infection, hemorrhage, inflammatory pneumonitis, and chronic interstitial lung disease. A useful point is that pulmonary edema usually has a more diffuse symmetric pattern and is often accompanied by pleural effusions or vascular congestion. However, the radiograph alone is not definitive, and an accurate diagnosis depends on clinical status and oxygenation.

Recommended next steps include assessment of hemodynamics, oxygen requirement, and cardiac status, with bedside echocardiography or urgent medical evaluation when acute pulmonary edema is suspected. Management is guided by the underlying cause, and radiographic follow-up can help confirm improvement in alveolar flooding after treatment.
