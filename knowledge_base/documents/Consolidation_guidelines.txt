Consolidation refers to replacement of air in the alveoli with fluid, inflammatory exudate, or cells, producing a region of increased opacity on chest radiography. Typical findings include homogeneous or patchy opacification that may have air bronchograms, often in a lobar or segmental distribution. Pneumonic consolidation is commonly associated with fever, cough, and focal symptoms, but aspiration or inflammatory processes can produce similar patterns.

The main differential diagnosis includes atelectasis, pulmonary edema, organizing pneumonia, and focal mass; distinguishing features such as volume loss, air bronchograms, and associated pleural effusion help guide interpretation. In some cases, collapsed lung appears denser but may show volume loss, whereas consolidation typically preserves or increases volume.

Recommended workup generally includes clinical assessment, microbiologic evaluation when infection is suspected, and repeat imaging when the appearance is atypical or the patient does not improve. Persistent focal consolidation should prompt consideration of malignancy, pulmonary abscess, organizing pneumonia, or alternative infectious etiologies, and may justify CT for further characterization.
