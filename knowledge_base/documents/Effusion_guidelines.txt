A pleural effusion is fluid in the pleural space, which on a frontal chest radiograph typically appears as a blunting of the costophrenic angle and, with larger volumes, a meniscus-shaped density layering in the lower hemithorax. Small effusions can be subtle, while larger effusions may cause mediastinal shift away from the affected side if the collection is substantial. The fluid may be transudative or exudative, and imaging cannot always differentiate etiology by appearance alone.

The differential diagnosis includes atelectasis, diaphragmatic elevation, pleural thickening, and less commonly a mass or consolidation that mimics fluid. Loculated effusions can form unusual shapes and may require ultrasound or CT to define the extent and loculation. If associated with pulmonary opacities, infection or malignancy should be considered in addition to simple fluid accumulation.

Recommended workup usually includes targeted history for heart failure, infection, malignancy, and trauma, followed by ultrasound when the fluid is uncertain or loculated. Thoracentesis is indicated when the effusion is new, unilateral, unexplained, or associated with significant symptoms, in order to establish the cause and guide treatment.
